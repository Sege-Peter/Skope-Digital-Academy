In secondary school, many of us copied notes from the board word for word. Those notes were often long, hard to revise from, and copied without much thinking. Good notes are different: they are short, in your own words, and built for **testing yourself later**.

## Why your own words matter

When you rewrite an idea in your own words, your brain has to understand it first. Copying word for word can happen without understanding anything. So:

- Listen or read a section first.
- Then write the main idea in a short sentence of your own.
- Add a quick example that makes sense to you.

## The Cornell method

A simple layout that builds in active recall. Divide your page into three parts:

```text
+------------------+-------------------------------------------+
| CUES / QUESTIONS | NOTES                                     |
|                  |                                           |
| What is a        | Budget = plan for how to use money        |
| budget?          | over a period (week/month).               |
|                  |                                           |
| 3 parts?         | Income, expenses, savings.                |
|                  | e.g. KES 8,000 income: rent 3,000,        |
| 50/30/20?        | food 2,500, transport 1,000, save 1,500   |
|                  | One common guide: 50% needs, 30% wants,   |
|                  | 20% savings (adjust to your situation).   |
+------------------+-------------------------------------------+
| SUMMARY: A budget shows where money goes. Plan income,       |
| expenses and savings. Track and adjust each month.           |
+--------------------------------------------------------------+
```

- **Right column:** your notes during the lesson.
- **Left column:** after the lesson, write questions or keywords for each note.
- **Bottom:** a two- or three-sentence summary.

To revise, cover the right side and answer the questions on the left. That is active recall built into your notebook.

## Mind maps

A **mind map** puts the main topic in the centre and branches out to sub-topics. It is useful for seeing how ideas connect, planning an essay or presentation, or summarising a whole module on one page. Use colours and small drawings if they help you.

## Paper or phone?

| Paper | Digital (Google Docs, Notes app, OneNote) |
|---|---|
| Fewer distractions | Easy to search and edit |
| Writing by hand can help you think and summarise | Can add links, screenshots and photos |
| Works without power or data | Backed up if you lose your book (when synced) |

Both work. Choose one main place so your notes are not scattered across five notebooks and three apps.

## Notes from videos

When learning from YouTube or online courses:

- Pause the video after each main point and write it in your own words.
- Note the timestamp of difficult parts so you can rewatch them.
- Do not copy everything on the screen. Ask "What is the one key idea here?"

> **Tip:** Review your notes within 24 hours, even for five minutes. Add cues and a summary if you have not already. This early review makes a big difference.

```exercise
Q: Fill in the blanks about the Cornell method.
In the left column you write ___ or keywords to test yourself.
At the bottom of the page you write a short ___.
A: questions / cues | summary
H: Things you ask yourself, and a brief wrap-up of the page.
```

## Practice task

Watch a short (10–15 minute) educational video on any skill you want to learn, for example a beginner Canva, Excel or budgeting tutorial. Take notes using the Cornell layout. The next day, cover the notes column and try to answer every question on the left.
