Employers and clients rarely ask for certificates first. They ask, "What have you built?" Your **portfolio** answers that question. In this final lesson you will plan a portfolio, learn where junior and freelance work comes from, and then take the course quiz.

## What a strong beginner portfolio contains

Three to five finished, working projects beat twenty half-done tutorials. Choose projects that solve believable local problems:

| Project idea | Shows that you can… |
|---|---|
| Website for a real local business (salon, bakery, church) | Build responsive pages a client would use |
| Booking or order form that saves to a database | Handle forms, validation and data |
| Chama contribution tracker | Plan requirements and build CRUD features (create, read, update, delete) |
| School results calculator | Write logic and tests |
| A team project with classmates | Use Git branches, pull requests and Scrum |

For each project, your GitHub README should include a screenshot, a live link (see the deployment lesson), the problem it solves, the tools you used and what you would improve next. Mention the process too: "I wrote user stories, drew wireframes and wrote 12 unit tests." That shows you understand the whole life cycle, not just code.

## Your online presence

- **GitHub:** pinned repositories, a profile README with a short introduction.
- **Portfolio page:** a simple one-page site with your name, skills, projects and contact details.
- **LinkedIn:** a clear photo, a headline such as "Junior Web Developer | HTML, CSS, JavaScript, PHP", and posts about what you are learning.

## Where the work comes from

| Route | How it works |
|---|---|
| Internships and attachments | Many Kenyan companies and tech hubs take interns; often the best first step |
| Junior job listings | LinkedIn, BrighterMonday, MyJobMag and company career pages |
| Local freelancing | Websites and systems for businesses, schools and SACCOs near you |
| Online freelancing | Upwork, Fiverr and similar platforms; start with small, well-defined jobs |
| Communities | Tech meetups, hackathons, Google Developer Groups and online communities, where people share openings |

Pay varies widely by skill, city and client. As an example only, a beginner might start with small paid fixes or simple websites while building experience, and raise prices as the portfolio grows. Agree on the price, deliverables and payment schedule in writing before starting, and consider asking for a deposit (for example, half up front) for new clients.

> **Warning:** Common scams target new developers: "jobs" that ask you to pay a registration or training fee, "clients" who send a cheque or extra M-Pesa and ask for a refund, and requests to log in to accounts with someone else's details. Never pay to get a job, and never share your passwords, tokens or M-Pesa PIN.

## Applying well

- Tailor each application: mention the company and how one of your projects relates to their work.
- Keep your CV to one or two pages, with projects and links near the top.
- In technical interviews, think out loud. Explaining your reasoning matters as much as the final answer.
- Follow up politely after a week if you hear nothing.

## Course wrap-up

You have walked through the whole journey of professional software: understanding the problem, writing requirements and user stories, planning with wireframes and flowcharts, tracking work with Git and GitHub, testing and debugging, working in Scrum teams, deploying, and presenting yourself to employers. Keep building small projects and repeat this cycle each time; that is how beginners become developers.

## Practice task

Write a plan for your portfolio: list three projects you will build (or improve) in the next two months, the live link and README you will create for each, and one job board or community you will check every week.
