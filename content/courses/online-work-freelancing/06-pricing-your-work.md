Pricing is one of the hardest parts of freelancing. Charge too little and you work long hours for almost nothing. Charge too much as a beginner with no reviews and nobody hires you. The aim is a **fair price that grows as your skill and reputation grow**.

## Two ways to charge

| Method | How it works | Best when |
|---|---|---|
| **Hourly** | You charge a rate per hour and track your time | The work is ongoing or hard to measure (admin, support) |
| **Fixed price** | One price for the whole job | The task is clear (a poster, 10 product descriptions) |

Many beginners prefer fixed prices because the client knows the total cost upfront. But for a fixed price, you must estimate your time carefully.

## Work out your minimum

Before you set a price, know the lowest amount that makes the job worth your time. Count your real costs:

- **Internet / data bundles**
- **Electricity** or phone charging
- **Tools** (for example a paid design app – many beginners use free versions)
- **Platform fees** – the platform keeps part of your earnings; check its current fee page
- **Withdrawal charges** – moving money to your bank or M-Pesa may cost something

### Example calculation

Brian is quoting for 10 product descriptions. He estimates 4 hours of work. He decides he wants at least KES 400 per hour for his time, and the job also uses about KES 100 in data.

```text
Time:   4 hours × KES 400 = KES 1,600
Costs:  data              = KES   100
Minimum price             = KES 1,700
```

He then adds a little extra for edits and the platform's fee, and quotes around KES 2,000. These numbers are only an example – your rate depends on your skill, the client and the market.

> **Note:** Fees charged by platforms, banks and mobile money change from time to time. Always check the current figures on the official website or app before you calculate.

## Research the market

Look at what other freelancers with **similar experience** charge for the same service. On Fiverr, search your service and look at gigs from sellers with few reviews. On Upwork, look at the budgets clients post for similar jobs. You will see a range – as a beginner, start somewhere in the lower-middle of that range, not the very bottom.

## Beginner pricing strategy

1. **Start fair, not free.** Very low prices attract clients who do not value your work.
2. **Raise your price after reviews.** After every few good reviews, increase your rate a little for new clients.
3. **Use packages.** Offer Basic, Standard and Premium options so clients can choose.
4. **Define the scope.** Write exactly what is included – number of pages, edits, delivery time.
5. **Charge for extras.** Extra edits, rush delivery or extra pages cost more.

| Package | What's included | Example price |
|---|---|---|
| Basic | 1 poster, 1 edit, 3-day delivery | KES 800 |
| Standard | 1 poster + WhatsApp status size, 3 edits, 2-day delivery | KES 1,500 |
| Premium | 3 posters, unlimited small edits, 1-day delivery | KES 3,500 |

*(Example figures only.)*

## Handling "Can you reduce?"

Kenyan clients – and international ones – often ask for a discount. Stay polite and protect your value: instead of cutting your price, **reduce the scope**. For example: "For KES 1,000 I can do the poster with one edit instead of three."

## Practice task

Choose one service you could sell. Estimate how many hours it takes, decide a minimum hourly figure for yourself, add your costs, and write **three packages** (Basic, Standard, Premium) with what each includes. Compare them with three similar gigs or job budgets online.
