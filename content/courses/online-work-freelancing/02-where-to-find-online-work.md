Once you know what skill you will offer, you need a place to meet clients. There are three main routes: big freelancing platforms, professional networks, and local connections. Most successful freelancers use more than one.

## Freelancing platforms

A platform is a website that connects clients with freelancers. The platform holds the payment, keeps a record of your work and lets clients leave reviews. In return it takes a service fee from what you earn.

| Platform | How it works | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| **Upwork** | Clients post jobs; you send proposals to apply. You use "Connects" to apply, and some cost money. | Writing, admin, design, tech, longer projects |
| **Fiverr** | You create "gigs" (ready-made services with a price); clients come to you and order. | Clear, packaged services like "I will design a poster" |
| **Freelancer.com** and similar | Clients post projects; freelancers bid. | Many small, competitive projects |

> **Note:** Platform fees, rules and the countries they accept change from time to time. Always read the current fee and payment pages on the platform's own website before you sign up. Do not trust fee figures you see in WhatsApp groups or old YouTube videos.

### Rules that protect you

Every platform has terms of service. Two rules matter a lot for beginners:

1. **Keep payments on the platform.** If a client asks you to move the job to WhatsApp and pay you outside the platform, you lose the platform's protection – and on some platforms you can lose your account.
2. **One person, one honest account.** Never buy, rent or "borrow" someone else's account. Accounts that are bought or shared are often closed, and the money in them can be frozen.

## LinkedIn

LinkedIn is a professional social network. It is not a freelancing platform, but many clients use it to find people. A clean LinkedIn profile with a good photo, a clear headline (for example "Virtual Assistant | Data Entry | Google Sheets") and samples of your work helps clients trust you. You can also find remote job posts in the **Jobs** section and follow companies you would like to work with.

## Local options

Do not ignore clients near you. Kenyan businesses need online help too, and they are often easier to reach for a first job.

- **Small businesses around you** – salons, hardware shops, schools, churches and restaurants often need posters, social media posts or typing work.
- **WhatsApp and Facebook groups** for business owners in your town.
- **Kenyan job boards** such as BrighterMonday, Fuzu and MyJobMag sometimes list remote and part-time roles.
- **Government programmes** – the Ajira Digital Programme has run training and information on online work for young Kenyans. Check the official site (ajiradigital.go.ke) for what is currently on offer.

> **Tip:** A local client who knows you can become your first review, your first portfolio sample and your first referral – all at once.

## Which route should you start with?

| If you… | Start with… |
|---|---|
| Have a clear, packaged skill (posters, logos, CVs) | Fiverr + local businesses |
| Can write good proposals and handle bigger tasks | Upwork |
| Want remote jobs with regular pay | LinkedIn + job boards |
| Have no reviews or samples yet | Local businesses and people you know |

Brian from Eldoret starts by designing menus for two cafés in town. He photographs the printed menus and adds them to his Fiverr gig. The real samples make his gig look trustworthy, and his first online order arrives a few weeks later.
