A proposal is your application for a job. Clients often receive dozens of proposals for one post, and most of them look the same: "Dear Sir/Madam, I am a hardworking freelancer with many years of experience…" The client stops reading after one line.

Your goal is simple: **show in the first two lines that you read their post and can solve their problem.**

## Read the job post carefully

Before writing anything, answer these questions from the post:

- What exactly does the client want done?
- What is the deadline?
- What tools or format do they mention (Google Docs, Canva, Excel)?
- Did they ask a question or include a test word? Some clients write "Start your proposal with the word *banana*" to check who actually read the post.

If you cannot answer these, read the post again – or skip it.

## A simple proposal structure

| Part | What to write | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Hook | Repeat their need in your own words and say you can do it | 1–2 sentences |
| 2. Proof | One relevant sample or experience, with a link | 1–2 sentences |
| 3. Plan | How you will do the job, step by step | 2–3 short points |
| 4. Question | One smart question about the project | 1 sentence |
| 5. Close | Availability and a friendly sign-off | 1 sentence |

## Example

Job post: *"Need someone to type 20 pages of handwritten notes into Google Docs. Must be accurate. Deadline Friday."*

```text
Hi Grace,

You need 20 pages of handwritten notes typed accurately into
Google Docs by Friday – I can deliver them by Thursday evening
so you have time to review.

Here is a sample of a typed document I prepared, with headings
and clean formatting: [link to sample]

My plan:
- Type the pages in order, keeping your headings and lists
- Mark any word I can't read with [?] so nothing is guessed
- Proofread everything once before sending

Quick question: would you like the notes in one document or
one document per chapter?

I am available to start today.
Brian Otieno
```

Notice what Brian did **not** write: his life story, "please give me a chance", or a list of twenty unrelated skills.

> **Tip:** Asking one good question shows you are thinking about the work. It also invites the client to reply – and a conversation is the first step to being hired.

## Common mistakes

- **Copy-paste proposals.** Clients can tell instantly. Keep a template for structure, but rewrite the hook and plan every time.
- **Begging.** "I really need this job" makes you sound desperate, not skilled.
- **Too long.** If it does not fit on a phone screen without much scrolling, cut it.
- **Spelling errors.** For writing and admin jobs, one mistake can end your chances. Read it twice.
- **Promising what you can't do.** Only apply for jobs you can actually complete on time.

## It is a numbers game – but a smart one

Even good proposals are often ignored. That is normal. Track your applications in a simple table so you can learn what works.

| Date | Job | Platform | Replied? | Hired? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 Mar | Type 20 pages | Upwork | Yes | Yes |
| 4 Mar | Product descriptions | Upwork | No | – |

If nobody replies after many proposals, change something: your hook, your samples or the type of job you apply for.

> **Warning:** If a "client" replies and immediately asks you to move to Telegram or WhatsApp, buy something, or pay for "training materials", stop. Real clients discuss the work first, on the platform.

## Practice task

Find **one real job post** for your skill on Upwork, LinkedIn or a job board. Without sending it, write a proposal using the five-part structure. Count the sentences in your hook – keep it to two. Then ask yourself: would *you* hire this person if you were the client?
