Mobile money is at the centre of daily life in Kenya, so it is also at the centre of fraud. The good news: almost every M-Pesa scam depends on **you** doing something – sending money, sharing your PIN or giving away a code. If you know the tricks, you can refuse.

## Common mobile money scams

| Scam | How it works | How to spot it |
|---|---|---|
| "Sent to wrong number" | You get a fake SMS saying someone sent you KES 3,000, then a call asking you to "send it back" | Check your **real M-Pesa balance** – the money never arrived. Real M-Pesa messages come from the official **MPESA** sender, not a phone number |
| Fake customer care | A caller claims to be from Safaricom and says your line will be blocked unless you follow instructions | Safaricom staff will never ask for your M-Pesa PIN |
| Fake promotion | "You have won KES 100,000! Send KES 1,500 to release your prize" | Real promotions never ask you to pay to receive a prize |
| Stranded relative | A message from a new number: "It's me, my phone fell. Send fare urgently" | Call the relative on their **known** number |
| Fake till or payment confirmation | A buyer shows you an edited screenshot as "proof of payment" | Release goods only after the money appears in **your own** M-Pesa or till statement |

> **Remember:** No genuine Safaricom, bank or Sacco employee needs your **M-Pesa PIN**, **bank PIN** or **one-time code (OTP)**. Anyone asking for them is a fraudster.

## What is SIM-swap fraud?

Your phone number is connected to your M-Pesa, bank alerts and the codes that protect your WhatsApp, email and social media. In **SIM-swap fraud**, a criminal gets your number moved onto a new SIM card that they control. They might do this by:

- Pretending to be you at a shop or agent, often with stolen ID details.
- Tricking you into revealing personal details over the phone first.
- Working with a dishonest insider.

Once they control your number, they receive your calls, SMS codes and password reset messages. They can then try to reset your M-Pesa PIN, empty mobile wallets linked to bank accounts, and take over your WhatsApp.

### Warning signs of a SIM swap

- Your phone suddenly shows **No service** or **SIM not registered** while others nearby have network.
- You stop receiving calls and SMS without explanation.
- You get a message about a SIM replacement you did not request.

### What to do immediately

1. Use another phone to call your network provider's customer care and ask them to **block the line** and stop any SIM replacement.
2. Call your bank and Sacco to freeze mobile banking.
3. Visit an official shop with your ID to restore your line.
4. Change passwords on important accounts once you have control again.
5. Report to the police (DCI) and get a reference number.

## Protecting yourself

- **Never share your PIN.** Cover the keypad when using it at an agent.
- **Use a strong M-Pesa PIN** – not your birth year, 1234 or 0000.
- **Protect your ID details.** Do not post photos of your ID, and be careful who you give copies to.
- **Limit what strangers know.** Fraudsters use your full name, ID number and date of birth to impersonate you.
- **Use app-based two-factor authentication** for important accounts (you will learn this in Module 2), so a stolen SIM alone is not enough.
- **Set a WhatsApp two-step verification PIN** – a separate PIN that WhatsApp asks for when your number is registered on a new phone.
- **Check messages using the official channels**: your M-Pesa app or menu, your bank app – never the number that called you.

> **Tip:** Safaricom subscribers can forward scam SMS messages to **333** and report fraudsters. Check safaricom.co.ke for the current fraud reporting options.

> **Warning:** Small business owners should confirm every payment in their own till or M-Pesa statement before handing over goods. Screenshots and forwarded messages can be faked in seconds.

## Practice task

Do these three things today:

1. Change your M-Pesa PIN if it is easy to guess.
2. Turn on WhatsApp two-step verification (**Settings** › **Account** › **Two-step verification**).
3. Tell one older relative about the "sent to wrong number" scam and how to check their real balance.
