Your email account is the master key to your digital life: if someone controls it, they can reset the passwords of almost everything else. Strong, unique passwords are the first lock on that door.

## How passwords get stolen

| Method | What happens |
|---|---|
| Guessing | Trying common passwords like `123456`, `password`, a name plus birth year |
| Phishing | You type your password into a fake login page |
| Data breaches | A website you used is hacked and its password list leaks online |
| Credential stuffing | Criminals take leaked email-and-password pairs and try them on other sites |
| Shoulder surfing | Someone watches you type in a cyber café or matatu |
| Malware | A keylogger on an infected computer records what you type |

Credential stuffing is why **reusing the same password** is dangerous. If a small online shop you used leaks your password, criminals will try it on your Gmail, Facebook and banking app the same day.

## What makes a password strong

1. **Length** matters most. Aim for **at least 12 characters**; longer is better.
2. **Unique** – a different password for every important account.
3. **Unpredictable** – not your name, phone number, school, team or birthday.

A great way to get length that you can remember is a **passphrase**: several random words joined together.

```text
Weak:      Brian2007
Weak:      Kenya@123
Better:    Gor!Mahia2025         (still based on guessable things)
Strong:    mango-ladder-Turkana-violin-42
Strong:    BluePikipikiSleepsUnderBaobab!
```

> **Tip:** A long passphrase of four or more unrelated words is both stronger and easier to remember than a short password full of symbols like `P@$$w0rd`.

## Password managers

Nobody can remember 30 unique passphrases. A **password manager** is an app that stores all your passwords in an encrypted vault, protected by one strong **master password**. It can also:

- **Generate** long random passwords for each site.
- **Fill them in** automatically – and only on the real website, which helps protect you from phishing pages.
- **Sync** between your phone and computer.
- **Warn** you about reused or leaked passwords.

| Option | Notes |
|---|---|
| Built into your browser or phone (Google Password Manager, Apple Passwords / iCloud Keychain) | Free and convenient; protected by your Google or Apple account |
| Dedicated apps (for example Bitwarden, which has a free plan) | Work across browsers and devices |

> **Warning:** Your master password and the account protecting your password manager must be very strong and protected with two-factor authentication. Never write it on a sticky note on your screen.

> **Note:** Do not save passwords in a browser on a **shared** computer such as a cyber café PC. When the browser asks "Save password?", choose **Never** and always **sign out** when you finish.

## Check if you have been in a breach

Websites such as **haveibeenpwned.com** let you enter your email address to see whether it appeared in known data breaches. If it has, change the password on that site and anywhere you reused it.

## Security questions and PINs

- Treat security questions like passwords. Your mother's maiden name or first school can often be found on Facebook. You can give a false but memorable answer and store it in your password manager.
- Phone and app PINs: avoid `0000`, `1234`, `1111` and your birth year.

## Practice task

1. List your five most important accounts (email, M-Pesa-linked bank app, WhatsApp backup account, Facebook/Instagram, school or work portal).
2. Change any reused or weak password among them to a passphrase.
3. Turn on your phone's built-in password manager or install a reputable one, and save these new passwords there.
