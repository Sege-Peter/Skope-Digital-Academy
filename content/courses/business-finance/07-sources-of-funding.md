Every business needs money to start and to grow. The question is *where* that money comes from and what it costs you. Some money is cheap but slow to gather; some is fast but expensive and risky.

## Main sources for small businesses in Kenya

| Source | How it works | Advantages | Risks and costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Personal savings** | Your own money, saved over time | No interest, no one to repay | Slow; you can lose it if the business fails |
| **Profits (bootstrapping)** | Reinvest part of each month's profit | No debt; growth matches demand | Growth is gradual |
| **Family and friends** | Gifts or informal loans | Flexible, often no interest | Can damage relationships if not repaid; agree terms in writing |
| **Chama** | A group saves together; members take turns or borrow from the pool | Discipline, social support, often lower costs | Depends on members being honest; rules must be clear and written |
| **SACCO** | A regulated savings and credit co-operative; loans are usually based on your savings and guarantors | Often cheaper than many other loans; builds a savings habit | You must save for a period first; guarantors are at risk if you default |
| **Bank or microfinance loan** | Formal loan with a contract | Larger amounts possible | Interest and fees; may need collateral or a track record |
| **Digital (mobile) loans** | Instant loans through apps or M-Pesa | Very fast and easy | Can be very expensive for short periods; late payment can lead to a negative credit listing |
| **Government and youth funds, grants, competitions** | Programmes for young entrepreneurs | May be cheap or free | Competitive; check that the programme is real |

> **Note:** Interest rates and fees change and differ between lenders. Do not rely on a figure from a friend or an old post – always ask the lender for the **total amount you will repay**, and check guidance from the Central Bank of Kenya ([centralbank.go.ke](https://www.centralbank.go.ke)).

## Always calculate the total cost

Before borrowing, write down: amount borrowed, total to repay, and the time.

```text
Example offer (illustrative numbers only)
Borrow:           KES 10,000
Total to repay:   KES 11,500 after 30 days
Cost of the loan: 11,500 − 10,000 = KES 1,500
```

Now ask: will this money earn *more* than KES 1,500 in profit within 30 days? If you are buying stock that you can sell quickly at a good margin, maybe. If you are paying rent because sales were slow, the loan only delays the problem and adds a cost.

## Good debt and bad debt (for a business)

- **Usually sensible:** borrowing for a proven need that will clearly earn more than it costs – for example, a second sewing machine when you are already turning away orders.
- **Usually dangerous:** borrowing to start an untested idea, to cover personal spending, or to repay another loan.

> **Warning:** Be careful of fake lenders who ask for an "insurance fee" or "processing fee" before releasing a loan. Real lenders deduct fees from the loan or include them in the repayment – they do not ask you to send money first to a personal number.

## Before you borrow – checklist

- I have tested the business and have records of sales.
- I know the **total** amount to repay and the dates.
- My cash flow forecast shows I can repay even in a slow month.
- I have read the terms, including penalties for late payment.
- I am not borrowing to repay another loan.

## Practice task

Visit or call **one SACCO or bank** near you (or check its official website) and find out: how long you must save before borrowing, what you need to apply, and how they show the total cost of a loan. Write down what you learn. Then list which funding source fits your business stage right now and why.
