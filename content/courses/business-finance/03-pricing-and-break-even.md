Now that you know your costs, you can set prices on purpose instead of guessing. This lesson covers three tools: **markup**, **profit margin** and the **break-even point**.

## Markup and margin

Brian bakes cakes in Nakuru. Each cake costs him KES 100 in ingredients, packaging and gas (variable cost). He sells each cake for KES 250.

- **Profit per cake** = 250 − 100 = KES 150
- **Markup** compares the profit with the *cost*: 150 ÷ 100 = 1.5 = **150%**
- **Profit margin** compares the profit with the *price*: 150 ÷ 250 = 0.6 = **60%**

| Term | Formula | Brian's cake |
|---|---|---|
| Markup | profit ÷ cost × 100 | 150% |
| Margin | profit ÷ selling price × 100 | 60% |

> **Tip:** People often mix up markup and margin. Margin is always smaller than markup for the same item. When a supplier says "you get a 30% margin", check which one they mean.

## Contribution per item

The money left from each sale after paying its variable cost is called the **contribution**. It "contributes" first to paying fixed costs, and after those are covered, to profit.

For Brian: contribution per cake = 250 − 100 = **KES 150**.

## Break-even point

The **break-even point** is the number of items you must sell in a period to cover all your costs – no profit, no loss.

```text
Break-even (units) = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution per unit
```

Brian's fixed costs are KES 6,000 a month (oven rent at a shared kitchen, and his phone bundle).

```text
Break-even = 6,000 ÷ 150 = 40 cakes per month
```

- Sell 40 cakes → profit is zero.
- Sell 60 cakes → profit = (60 − 40) × 150 = **KES 3,000**.
- Sell 30 cakes → loss = (40 − 30) × 150 = **KES 1,500**.

If the answer is not a whole number, round **up**, because you cannot sell part of a cake.

## Using break-even to make decisions

| Question | What break-even tells you |
|---|---|
| Is this price realistic? | If you must sell 500 a month to break even but can only sell 100, change the price or costs. |
| Should I rent a bigger shop? | Higher rent raises fixed costs, so you must sell more just to break even. |
| Should I offer a discount? | A lower price shrinks contribution, so break-even rises. |

## Prices and the market

Numbers tell you the *lowest* sensible price. The market tells you the *highest*. Check what competitors charge and what customers value – speed, quality, delivery, trust. Then choose a price between the two.

```exercise
Q: An item costs KES 200 and sells for KES 500. Work out the profit per item and the margin.
Profit = KES ___, margin = ___%
A: 300 | 60
H: Margin = profit ÷ selling price × 100.
```

```exercise
Q: Fixed costs are KES 9,000 a month and contribution is KES 300 per item. Find the break-even point.
Break-even = ___ items
A: 30
```

## Practice task

Using your list of costs from last week, calculate the **contribution per unit** and the **break-even point** for one product or service. Then write down: is it realistic to sell that many in a month? If not, what would you change – price, costs or both?
