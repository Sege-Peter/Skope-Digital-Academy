Many people wait years to "raise capital". Most successful small businesses in Kenya started with very little and grew from their own profits. Starting small keeps your risk low while you learn.

## Ways to start with little capital

- **Use what you already have** – your phone, skills, kitchen, bicycle or laptop.
- **Pre-orders** – take payment or a deposit before buying stock.
- **Buy small, sell, then buy again** – turn your stock over quickly instead of buying a lot at once.
- **Borrow or share equipment** – rent a sewing machine by the day, share a stall with a friend.
- **Start part-time** – keep your other work or studies while the business proves itself.

> **Warning:** Avoid starting with a big loan, especially a quick mobile loan. If the business is slow, the repayments still come. Test first, borrow later (if ever) and only for something that will clearly earn back more than it costs.

## List your start-up costs

```text
Start-up list – Brian's chapati business
Large pan (second-hand)     KES 800
Rolling pin and board       KES 350
Cool box to carry food      KES 1,200
First flour, oil, beans     KES 1,150
Total                       KES 3,500
```

Separate **must-have** items from **nice-to-have** ones. A branded apron can wait.

## Setting your price

Your price must cover your costs **and** leave a profit. Use three checks:

1. **Cost check** – what does one unit cost you to make or buy?
2. **Market check** – what do customers pay others for something similar?
3. **Value check** – is your product better, faster or closer than the others?

### Cost-plus pricing

Work out the cost per unit, then add a markup.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Ingredients for 40 chapatis | KES 600 |
| Charcoal and transport | KES 200 |
| Total cost for 40 | KES 800 |
| Cost per chapati (800 ÷ 40) | KES 20 |
| Price chosen | KES 30 |
| Profit per chapati (30 − 20) | KES 10 |

If Brian sells all 40, his profit is 40 × 10 = **KES 400**. If he sells only 30, he collects 30 × 30 = KES 900, which is still more than his KES 800 cost – but his profit falls to KES 100.

> **Tip:** Do not forget your own time. If a job takes you three hours, the price should reward those hours, not just pay for materials.

## Common pricing mistakes

- Pricing lower than everyone else "to attract customers" and never making a profit.
- Forgetting small costs like transport, packaging and M-Pesa charges.
- Never reviewing prices when the cost of flour or fuel goes up.
- Giving too much credit ("nitakulipa Friday") to friends.

```exercise

```

## Practice task

Write your **start-up list** with real prices from a shop or online, and mark each item as must-have or nice-to-have. Then use cost-plus pricing to work out the cost per unit of your product or service and choose a price. Check it against what two competitors charge.
