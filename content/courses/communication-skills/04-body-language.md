People read your body before they hear your words. A customer walking into a shop decides in seconds whether the attendant looks welcoming. Your posture, face, eyes and hands all send messages, often without you noticing.

## Why it matters

When your words and your body say different things, people tend to believe your body. If you say "I'm happy to help" while frowning and looking at your phone, the customer will not feel welcome.

## The main signals

| Signal | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Face | Natural smile, relaxed | Frowning, rolling eyes |
| Eyes | Comfortable eye contact | Staring, or never looking up |
| Posture | Upright, shoulders relaxed | Slouching, leaning on the counter |
| Arms | Open, hands visible | Tightly crossed, hands in pockets |
| Distance | Comfortable personal space | Standing too close |
| Movement | Calm gestures | Fidgeting, tapping, checking phone |

## Eye contact and culture

In many Kenyan homes, young people are taught not to look elders directly in the eye as a sign of respect. In most workplaces and interviews, however, comfortable eye contact is read as **confidence and honesty**. A good balance:

- Look at the person's face while they speak and while you answer.
- Glance away naturally every few seconds so it does not become a stare.
- With elders or in community settings, be guided by what is respectful there.

## Handshakes and greetings

A greeting sets the tone. In professional settings a handshake is common: firm but not crushing, with eye contact and a smile. Follow the other person's lead; some people prefer not to shake hands for religious, cultural or health reasons, and a warm verbal greeting is perfectly fine.

## Your own nervous signals

When nervous, people often:

- Touch their face or hair.
- Shake a leg or tap a pen.
- Speak while looking at the floor.

Notice your habits by recording a mock interview or presentation on your phone. Once you see them, you can replace them: place your hands calmly on the table, plant both feet on the floor, and look up.

## Reading other people

Body language works both ways. Watch for signals in others:

- A customer looking at the door or their watch may be in a hurry; get to the point.
- Someone with crossed arms and a tight face may be unhappy; ask gently, "Is everything okay with the order?"
- A colleague nodding and leaning in is interested; keep going.

> **Note:** One signal alone does not prove anything. Crossed arms might just mean the room is cold. Look for several signals together.

## Practice task

Stand in front of a mirror (or record a video) and greet an imaginary customer twice: once with closed, bored body language, and once with open, welcoming body language. Notice the difference. Then, next time you are in a shop or office, observe the staff and write down three positive and three negative signals you see.
